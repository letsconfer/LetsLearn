French Pronouns & Essential Verbs

Deep Dive into Subject Pronouns, Elision, Reflexive Verbs, and Verbs: Être, Avoir, Aller, & Faire

1. French Pronouns (Les Pronoms)

French: Les Pronoms / Pronunciation: ले प्रोनॉँ

Definition: Pronouns are words such as I, he, she, and they that perform the action expressed by the verb. Pronouns stand in for nouns when it is clear who is being talked about (e.g., My brother isn’t here at the moment. He’ll be back in an hour.).

Subject Pronouns (Les Pronoms Sujets):
• 1st Person Singular: Je (I) / Elision Rule: When Je is followed by a word starting with a vowel or mute 'h', it drops 'e' and becomes J' (e.g., j'ai, j'habite).
• 2nd Person Singular: Tu (You - informal / singular).
• 3rd Person Singular: Il (He / It - masculine), Elle (She / It - feminine), On (One / We - informal singular/general pronoun).
• 1st Person Plural: Nous (We).
• 2nd Person Plural: Vous (You - formal singular 'aap' / plural 'aap sab').
• 3rd Person Plural: Ils (They - masculine or mixed group), Elles (They - exclusively feminine group).

Regular vs. Irregular Verbs (Les Verbes Réguliers et Irréguliers)

In French, verbs are broadly categorized into regular and irregular verbs based on how they conjugate in different tenses.

1. Regular Verbs (Les Verbes Réguliers)

Explanation: Regular verbs follow a fixed, predictable pattern. They are divided into three main groups based on their infinitive endings:

  • 1st Group (-er): parler, aimer, regarder
  • 2nd Group (-ir): finir, choisir, grandir
  • 3rd Group (-re): vendre, attendre, répondre

To conjugate a regular verb in the present tense, drop the ending (e.g., -er) and add standard personal endings for each pronoun (-e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent).

Example (-er verb: parler – to speak):
  • Je parle [zhuh pahrl] (झ़् पार्ल) — I speak / मैं बोलता हूँ
  • Tu parles [too pahrl] (त्यू पार्ल) — You speak / तुम बोलते हो
  • Nous parlons [noo pahr-lohng] (नू पार्लों) — We speak / हम बोलते हैं

2. Irregular Verbs (Les Verbes Irréguliers)

Explanation: Irregular verbs do not follow standard rules. Their stems change, or they use unique set endings that must be memorized individually. Most irregular verbs belong to the 3rd group (e.g., être, avoir, aller, faire, venir).

Example (Irregular verb: être – to be):
  • Je suis [zhuh swee] (झ़् स्वी) — I am / मैं हूँ
  • Tu es [too ay] (त्यू ए) — You are / तुम हो
  • Il/Elle est [eel / el ay] (इल / एल ए) — He/She is / वह है
  • Nous sommes [noo suhm] (नू सुम) — We are / हम हैं
  • Vous êtes [voo zet] (वू ज़ेत) — You are / आप हैं
  • Ils/Elles sont [eel / el sohng] (इल / एल सोँ) — They are / वे हैं

3. The Verb Être (To Be) - Present Indicative

French: Verbe être - Indicatif présent / Pronunciation: वेर्ब एत्र - अँदीकातिफ प्रेजाँ

You almost never see the actual word "être" written in a normal sentence, just like you rarely see the word "to be" written in an English sentence.

Instead, it changes its shape depending on who is doing the action.

Here is exactly how it works:

1. It is the "Parent Word" (The Infinitive)
In English, if I ask you, "What is the parent word for 'am', 'is', and 'are'?" the answer is "to be."
You don't say "I to be happy." You change it to "I am happy."
Être is simply the French version of "to be." It is the name of the verb, but it changes its face when you use it.

2. How you actually use it in a sentence?
When you want to use it, you must swap the word être for its real-life shapes:
• Instead of "Je être", you say: Je suis (I am)
• Instead of "You être", you say: Tu es (You are)
• Instead of "He être", you say: Il est (He is)

Example:

• English: I am American. (You didn't use the word "be", you used "am").
• French: Je suis américain. (You didn't use the word "être", you used "suis").

3. When do you use the actual word "être"?
Most Important: You only use the exact word être when it follows another verb, meaning "to be."
• Je veux être riche. = I want to be rich.
• Il aime être seul. = He likes to be alone.

💡 The Ultimate Summary:
• What is it?
It is just the vocabulary word for "to be."

• Do we use it in a sentence?
Usually no. You break it apart into suis, es, est, sommes, êtes, sont, just like English breaks "to be" into am, is, are.

Possessive Adjectives ("My"):
• Mon = Masculine singular (e.g., mon frère, mon père, mon ami)
• Ma = Feminine singular (e.g., ma sœur, ma mère, ma maison)
• Mes = Plural for both genders (e.g., mes amis, mes parents, mes livres)

4. The Verb Avoir (To Have) - Present Indicative

French: Verbe avoir - Indicatif présent / Pronunciation: वेर्ब आव्वार - अँदीकातिफ प्रेजाँ

Just like être, the verb avoir (to have) is a chameleon word. You rarely see the raw word "avoir" in a sentence. Instead, it changes its shape depending on who is doing the action.

It is the "parent word" for has and have.

Here is the breakdown of avoir in the present tense (Présent de l'indicatif).

1. The Present Tense Shapes (Conjugation)
Because avoir is highly irregular, you must memorize its shapes. Notice how almost all of them start with letter A.

2. How You Actually Use It in a Sentence
You swap out the parent word avoir for one of its live shapes to show possession:

Example: I have a car.
You don't say Je avoir une voiture.
You use the "I" shape: J'ai une voiture.

3. When do you use the actual word "avoir"?
Just like être, you only use the raw word avoir when it comes right after another verb, meaning "to have":
Example: Je veux avoir un chat. (I want to have a cat.)

🚨 The French "Avoir" Traps (Very Important !)
In English, we use "to be" (am/is/are) for physical feelings and age.

French completely switches this and uses avoir (to have) instead.

If you use être here, it will sound very strange to a French speaker!
• Age: J'ai 20 ans. (Literal: I have 20 years. English: I am 20 years old.)
• Hunger: J'ai faim. (Literal: I have hunger. English: I am hungry.)
• Thirst: J'ai soif. (Literal: I have thirst. English: I am thirsty.)
• Cold/Hot: J'ai froid. / J'ai chaud. (English: I am cold / I am hot.)
• Fear: J'ai peur. (English: I am afraid.)

5. The Verb Aller (To Go) - Irregular Verb

French: Verbe aller - Indicatif présent / Pronunciation: वेर्ब आल्ले - अँदीकातिफ प्रेजाँ

Definition: Aller means "to go". It is one of the most common irregular verbs in French and is also used to form near-future tense sentences.

Detailed Explanation:

Just like être and avoir, the verb aller (to go) is a massive chameleon word in French.
Even though it ends in -er (like regular verbs), it is completely irregular. It changes its face so dramatically in the present tense that the word aller vanishes entirely, replaced by words starting with V!

Here is your comprehensive, easy-to-digest guide to mastering aller.

1. Present Tense Conjugation (Présent de l'indicatif)
You must memorize these shapes by heart. Notice how the singular forms completely switch to the letter V

2. Dual Purpose: How We Use "Aller"?

A. Physical Movement (Going places)
You swap the raw word aller for a shape to say where you are heading.
• Je vais à la plage. = I am going to the beach.
• Ils vont au cinéma. = They are going to the cinema.

B. The "Near Future" Hack (Le Futur Proche)
This is the ultimate conversational cheat code. You can talk about the future without learning any complex future tense endings. Just use Shape of Aller + Any Parent Word (Infinitive):
• Je vais manger. = I am going to eat.
• Tu vas parler. = You are going to speak.
• Nous allons voyager. = We are going to travel.

🧠 Cheat Sheet & Remember Tips
💡 Tip 1: The "Boot" Rule (The V-to-Aller split)
Draw a boot over your conjugation chart (Je, Tu, Il, Ils).
• Inside the boot (Singulars + They): The word completely mutates into a V word (vais, vas, va, vont).
• Outside the boot (Nous, Vous): The words stay loyal to the parent stem all- (allons, allez).

💡 Tip 2: The "Ils Vont" Warning 🚨
Students constantly mix up Ils ont (They have - from avoir) and Ils vont (They go - from aller).
• Remember: Vont starts with V for Vacation (because you go on vacation!).

💡 Tip 3: Asking "How are you?"
In French, you don't ask "How are you?" (using être). You ask "How are you going?" using aller:
• Comment ça va ? = How's it going?
• Je vais bien. = I am doing well (Literally: I am going well).

Preposition Rules with Destinations (Aller + Place):
• au + Masculine singular place/country (e.g., au Canada, au Japon, au théâtre, au zoo, au restaurant, au parc).
• en + Feminine singular country or starting with vowel (e.g., en Inde, en France, en Italie).
• à la + Feminine singular place (e.g., à la piscine, à la banque, à la plage).
• à l' + Place starting with vowel/mute h (e.g., à l'école, à l'hôpital, à l'aéroport).
• à + City name (e.g., à Paris, à Delhi, à Amritsar).
• aux + Plural place/country (e.g., aux États-Unis, aux Pays-Bas, aux toilettes).

6. The Verb Faire (To Do / To Make)

French: Verbe faire - Indicatif présent / Pronunciation: वेर्ब फेर - अँदीकातिफ प्रेजाँ
Detailed Explanation:

The verb faire (to do / to make) is the fourth and final giant of conversational French.

Just like être, avoir, and aller, faire is a major chameleon word. You won't see the raw word faire in most sentences; instead, it breaks apart into unique shapes. In English, we use two separate words ("do" and "make"), but French elegantly combines them both into this one single verb!

1. Present Tense Conjugation (Présent de l'indicatif)
You must memorize these shapes by heart. Pay close attention to the Nous, Vous, and Ils forms, as they have highly irregular endings that catch many students off guard

2. Dual Purpose: How We Use "Faire"

A. Creating or Performing Actions (To Do / To Make)
You swap the raw word faire for a shape to show what you are physically crafting or performing.
• Je fais un gâteau. = I am making a cake.
• Elle fait ses devoirs. = She is doing her homework.

B. Activities, Hobbies, and Sports
In English, we say "I play tennis" or "I go cooking." In French, you use shape of faire + de (du/de la/des) for almost all sports, instruments, and hobbies:
• Nous faisons du tennis. = We play tennis.
• Ils font de la guitare. = They play the guitar.
• Tu fais la cuisine. = You do the cooking.

🧠 Cheat Sheet & Remember Tips
💡 Tip 1: The "Vous" Trap 🚨
99% of French verbs end in -ez when pairing with Vous (e.g., vous allez, vous avez). Faire is one of the very few exceptions. It ends in -tes (Vous faites).
• Memory Trick: Remember that Vous faites rhymes with Vous êtes (from être). They are irregular buddies!

💡 Tip 2: The Weather Watch ☀️❄️
In English, we use "to be" to talk about the weather ("It is hot", "It is beautiful"). French uses faire because the weather "makes" an environment:
• Il fait beau. = It is beautiful weather.
• Il fait froid. = It is cold.

💡 Tip 3: "Nous Faisons" Pronunciation Trick
Even though it is spelled with an "ai" (which usually sounds like ay), the ai in faisons is uniquely pronounced like the weak "uh" sound in the French word je. Think of it sounding like "fuh-zohn."

Here are the only two times you will use the raw word aller in a sentence (How You Actually Use It in a Sentence):
Rule 1: Right After Another Verb (The "Verb Sandwich")
When two verbs sit right next to each other in a sentence, only the first verb changes its shape. The second verb must stay as its raw "parent word" (the infinitive).
• Je veux aller en France. = I want to go to France.
(The first verb "veux" changes, so "aller" stays exactly as it is).
• Il aime aller au parc. = He likes to go to the park.

Rule 2: In the Near Future Tense (Le Futur Proche)
Remember the near future hack from earlier? (Shape of Aller + Any Parent Word). If you are "going to go" somewhere, you pair a changed shape of aller with the raw word aller right after it!
• Je vais aller au cinéma. = I am going to go to the cinema.
• Nous allons aller au restaurant. = We are going to go to the restaurant.

The "-ont" Club

Important Legands

* The Hack for Avoir and Aller:
 Singular forms: Aller is just Avoir with a V slapped onto the front.
For Nous and Vous, they both remember their infinitives (Av-oir and All-er) and become completely regular (avons/avez and allons/allez).
The Plural form: They meet back up in the plural form with the "-ont" ending (ont ➔ vont).

** The Hack for Faire:
 Singular forms: The root is fais- across the board. Just add standard endings: -s, -s, -t (fais, fais, fait).
For Nous: Keeps the root fais- and adds the standard regular ending (faisons). Note: It is pronounced "feh-zohn" but spelled fais-.
For Vous: People always get tripped up here by writing faisez. Don't. Faire joins Être as the only verbs that use -tes (vous êtes / vous faites).
The Plural form: Ils: Dops the 's' from the root and joins the "-ont" club (font).
